## Q1. Redwood Research (and Redwood-adjacent control papers): what exactly do they threshold, and how?

### Takeaway
Redwood's evaluation rule is a fixed audit budget turned into a score threshold at the (1 − budget) quantile of honest-model monitor scores. The budget is 2% of problems in the 2023 paper and 1% of actions or steps in the agentic papers. So their detectors are Neyman–Pearson-style at heart. But Redwood was also the first group to run things on top of that rule: time-varying (exponentially decaying) audit thresholds, a within-trajectory "three strikes" count rule, resampling to gather more evidence (SPRT-like in spirit), a binomial false-positive-rate shutdown test, and a Bayesian credence that carries across tasks (Wen et al.). The newer 2025–2026 benchmark papers (BashArena, LinuxArena, Factor(T,U)) went back to a plain constant threshold with max-over-steps aggregation.

### Cited Findings

**AI Control (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan, Roger; Dec 2023, ICML 2024)** — [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- Unit: per APPS solution (one code submission per task). Episodes have 5,000 problems. The trusted monitor is GPT-3.5, and the score comes from "a combination of GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-3.5-Turbo-Instruct for log probabilities." So the score is logprob-based. [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- Threshold: "We pick a classifier threshold such that the classifier has a 2% false positive rate on the distribution of solutions written by GPT-4". Trusted monitoring = "Audit the top 2% most suspicious code". Defer-to-trusted adds a second, lower threshold: "suspicion is between top 2% and top 10%". They justify knowing the honest distribution: blue team may use honest-score summary statistics (Sec. 2.3). [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- Time-variation was explicitly suppressed: "First 10 problems are audited for free: In practice, auditing earlier problems is better than auditing later problems. We didn't want to figure out the optimal weighting toward earlier problems… This reduces the incentive to implement approaches which adapt auditing thresholds over the course of the scenario." [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- A sequential false-positive-rate test across tasks (Appendix G), to stop the attacker from draining the audit budget with false positives: "the protocol also continuously checks to see if there have been significantly more false positives from monitoring than the expected 2% rate (tested using the binomial CDF with p = 0.03). When there are significantly more false positives than expected, we only submit solutions from the trusted model… then swap back… when there are no longer significantly more false positives." This is a running count test over the deployment, with reversible switching (hysteresis-like). [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- Untrusted monitoring with upfront validation: the protocol spends 30 audit points on synthetic backdoors and shuts down "if the false negative rate is too high". It then audits "the top 1.4% most suspicious proposals". It also shuts down "if the false positive rate of our combined suspicion score is much higher than expected". Trusted and untrusted scores are ensembled (Appendix K). [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)
- Metric: safety (P[blue team wins]) vs usefulness Pareto curves. [arXiv 2312.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)

**Games for AI Control (Griffin, Thomson, Shlegeris, Abate; Sep 2024)** — [arXiv 2409.07985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07985)
- Formalizes trusted monitoring as a game. The blue team sees (m, c, x): m = problems remaining, c = audit "coins" remaining, x = monitor score. Their "memoryless threshold protocols" are "characterised by the false positive rates (FPRs) for ⟨Audit⟩ and ⟨Defer⟩ (q^a_{m,c}, q^d_{m,c}), for each m, c. Let g(q) be the threshold that gives an FPR of q". So in the formalism the threshold may depend on time and on remaining budget. It is still an FPR quantile of the honest score distribution, with no memory of past scores. [arXiv 2409.07985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07985)
- Finding: an audit FPR above the budget-matched 2% helps. "Choosing an audit FPR of 3% gives a small Pareto-improvement… and an audit FPR of 5%, gives a large Pareto-improvement in the 'weak' Trusted Monitoring case." This happens despite "increasing the probability that the audit budget is exhausted early". [arXiv 2409.07985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07985)

**Adaptive Deployment of Untrusted LLMs Reduces Distributed Threats (Wen, Hebbar, Larson, Bhatt, … Shlegeris; Nov 2024, ICLR 2025)** — [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17693)
- Low-stakes / "distributed threats" setting. A two-level design: per-task micro-protocols, plus a macro-protocol that "maintains an adaptive credence on the untrusted model's alignment over the course of the entire task sequence" (Bayesian evidence carried across tasks). [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17693)
- Update rule, done: "The macro-protocol performs a Bayesian update… c^j_t = c^j_{t−1} P(s_t | h^j) / Σ_k c^k_{t−1} P(s_t | h^k)". Hypotheses h = (r, β) give a misbehavior rate and attack-selection parameters. P(s_t | h) is estimated from a "reference distribution" by bucketing monitor scores, with an additive smoothing term a. The protocol then picks the micro-protocol output that maximizes expected utility, "1_benign·usefulness − α·harmfulness". [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17693)
- The prior is a Nash-equilibrium prior that is "optimal" for worst-case evaluation. To avoid overconfidence ("errors can accumulate over long time sequences to produce overconfident credences"), they bootstrap over subsamples of the reference set. [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17693)
- Baselines are explicitly static ("to demonstrate the importance of dynamic thresholding"): Static-EV (the credence is frozen at the prior) and Static-Threshold ("a static safety threshold η… to filter micro-protocols' outputs"). Result: the adaptive protocol "already outperforms static baselines at t = 10… and it continuously improves over time". [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17693)

**Ctrl-Z: Controlling AI Agents via Resampling (Bhatt, Rushing, Kaufman, Tracy, … Shlegeris; Apr 2025)** — [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Setting: BashBench, 257 multi-step sysadmin tasks, up to 15 steps per task, in an episode of 100,000 tasks. The trusted monitor is Llama-3.1-8B, which sees "all of the actions that have been taken so far in the trajectory" when scoring a step. [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Budget: audits may cover a number "equivalent to 1% of the actions taken in an episode". "The 1% metric was chosen arbitrarily: auditing 1% is higher than what we expect to be able to afford in real deployment." [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Time-varying thresholds, done: "For tasks earlier in the episode, we use a lower auditing threshold… and for later tasks, a higher threshold. This method, which we call exponential auditing budgeting, provides a significant safety improvement compared to the uniform auditing allocation in Greenblatt et al. 2023." With trusted monitoring, safety is 41.8% using exponential budgeting vs 22% with "a uniform auditing technique… which holds the threshold constant". The decay rate is numerically optimized against a best-responding red team (Appendix L). [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Tiered thresholds, done: the defer threshold is much lower than the audit threshold, because deferring costs only usefulness while auditing costs scarce human time. [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Resampling as evidence gathering, done. Defer to resample: if a step's score exceeds the defer threshold, "we generate up to two new samples from U" and execute any sample under the threshold. Defer to resample with incrimination: "the protocol samples up to four more actions. If the minimum of the samples' suspiciousness scores hits the auditing threshold, the protocol audits". A resampled step's score is "the minimum of suspiciousness scores among its samples". [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Within-trajectory evidence accumulation, done. "Auditing after three strikes: Within a task, the blue team keeps a record of how many steps' suspiciousness scores were above a certain threshold. Once this happens for the third time, it audits the trajectory. Additionally, there is a higher threshold where the blue team audits if the suspiciousness of the current step hits that threshold". "Empirically, we find that 3 is the best number of strikes." "Time travel (Incrimination in the past)" resamples "the third most suspicious past step" when a trajectory is near the three-strikes threshold. [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- Result: at 95% usefulness, safety rises from 72.7% (defer to trusted) to 87.1% (time travel). Metric: safety vs usefulness. [arXiv 2504.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10374)
- A later critique: resampling can backfire against prompt-injection attacks, because "its resampling amplifies the prompt injection and effectively reframes it as a best-of-n attack". [Terekhov et al., arXiv 2510.09462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09462)

**BashArena (Kaufman, Lucassen, Tracy, Rushing, …; Dec 2025)** — [arXiv 2512.15688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15688)
- 637 tasks and 4 side tasks. Monitor: before each action, it sees the task, "all four side task descriptions", and all tool calls and outputs so far, but not the CoT or system prompt. It rates "how suspicious the tool calls in the current action are on a scale from 0 to 10". The GPT-4.1-mini monitor takes "three suspicion scores… and take[s] the average". [arXiv 2512.15688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15688)
- Threshold: "we use a trajectory-wise FPR of 4% to compute safety, which corresponds to a step-wise FPR of 0.08%". FPR is computed on Claude Sonnet 4.5 honest runs for every attacker. Aggregation is max over actions: Sonnet "gave all attack trajectories a maximum 9/9 suspicion score on at least one action". [arXiv 2512.15688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15688)
- Discreteness problem: "Because the monitors rate trajectories on a 0-10 scale, their performance with very low FPR is poor without ensembling… because it also gave 9 out of 9,894 actions from honest runs a 9/9, it cannot detect all of the attacks if the required FPR is lower than 0.09% of actions." Suggested, not done: "sampling additional ratings in the small fraction of cases where it is highly suspicious" and "monitors will likely be trained to output calibrated continuous scores". [arXiv 2512.15688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15688)

**LinuxArena (Tracy, Potham, Kuhn, Heller, … Redwood; Apr 2026)** — [arXiv 2604.15384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15384)
- Two monitors. A per-action ("live") monitor scores 0–10 before execution. A full-trajectory ("delayed") monitor scores 0–10 afterwards. "we take the mean score of two independent monitor calls". [arXiv 2604.15384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15384)
- Threshold: "At 1% step-wise FPR the threshold sits at the 99th percentile of the honest per-action score distribution, with probabilistic tie-breaking to hit the target rate exactly. A trajectory counts as flagged if any of its actions is flagged" (max / any-step rule). For live-failure side tasks only actions before the harm step count. "The threshold is calibrated per attacker against its model-matched honest run." [arXiv 2604.15384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15384)
- Combined live and delayed rule (appendix): "c(a) = max(r_L(a), r_D(T))". These are percentile ranks, and the threshold is "the (1 − FPR)-quantile of the honest per-action c distribution". [arXiv 2604.15384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15384)
- Headline: the GPT-5 Nano trusted monitor misses 34% of Opus sabotage at 1% step-wise FPR. [LinuxArena search summary / arXiv 2604.15384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15384); [Redwood blog](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/introducing-linuxarena)

**Factor(T,U) (Sandoval, Rushing; Dec 2025)** — [arXiv 2512.02157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02157)
- The blue team is deliberately constrained to "maintaining a constant audit threshold for all tasks and give no free audits". The threshold is discretized ("threshold is set at 8"), and solutions exactly on the threshold are "audited at random, with a fixed on-threshold audit probability set such that the budget is met in expectation". [arXiv 2512.02157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02157)

**Control Tax (Terekhov, Liu, Gulcehre, …; Jun 2025; EPFL/MATS, Redwood-style)** — [arXiv 2506.05296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05296)
- Theory: "rearranging any finite auditing strategy into one with a non-increasing budget as a function of time results in safety at least as good as the original strategy". "Auditing p(τ) samples per round corresponds to selecting time-dependent thresholds". This "aligns with… exponentially decaying allocations introduced by Bhatt et al. (2025)". The paper uses a 1–100 suspiciousness scale and reports results for "optimal sequential monitoring with a 5% fixed auditing budget". [arXiv 2506.05296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05296)

**Combining Cost-Constrained Runtime Monitors (Hua, Baskerville, Lemoine, Hopman, Bhatt, Tracy; Jul 2025)** — [arXiv 2507.15886](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15886)
- An explicit Neyman–Pearson framing, done: "We model the auditing decision as a hypothesis testing problem between H0: B = 0 (aligned) and H1: B = 1 (misaligned)… the likelihood ratio test". Likelihoods are estimated by KDE. "audit all outputs with likelihood ratios exceeding this threshold". The NP policies "outperform the Π_naive policy class from Greenblatt et al." The unit is a single output. Costs are per monitor call ($0.000001 for a probe, $1 for a black-box monitor, $20 for an audit). [arXiv 2507.15886](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15886); [LW summary](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/FhixwyymPxF8TZX39/optimally-combining-probe-monitors-and-black-box-monitors)

**Logit ROCs (Staley, Bhatt, Stastny; Redwood blog, Apr 12 2026)** — [Redwood blog](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/logit-rocs-monitor-tpr-is-linear)
- 0–100 scores. TPR = "fraction of attack trajectories where at least one message scores above some threshold" (max rule). FPR = "fraction of benign messages scoring above the corresponding threshold". Realistic budgets are "e.g. 1e-4, that is 1 audit for every 10,000 actions". Claim: the low-FPR ROC "tends to be linear on a logit-logit plot". This lets one extrapolate TPR to deployment FPRs. [Redwood blog](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/logit-rocs-monitor-tpr-is-linear)

**Attack-selection papers (2026)**
- Concentrated (APPS-style): "The audit threshold is defined by the 100 − h percentile of the monitor suspicious scores for the honest" policy, where h is the audit budget. [arXiv 2602.04930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04930)
- Agentic (BashArena / LinuxArena): the evaluation uses "the top 1% of trajectories by" suspicion as the audit budget, with monitor "samples per agent action on a 0–10 suspicion scale". At a 1% budget, attack selection cuts LinuxArena safety by ~20 pp, "from 0.36". [arXiv 2606.06529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06529)

### Inferences
- Redwood's evaluation primitive is consistently a Neyman–Pearson-type test with a fixed budget: threshold = honest quantile at FPR = audit budget, with max (any-step) aggregation inside a trajectory. Their deviations from that are protocol-level and not detector-level: time-varying budget, strikes, resampling, credences. Each is justified by game-theoretic best response, not by change-point or CUSUM theory.
- "Three strikes" is a count-of-exceedances statistic (k-of-n), and the min-over-resamples rule is a crude repeated-measurement test. Both resemble sequential testing but have no formal error control.
- The 2025–2026 benchmark papers (BashArena, LinuxArena, Factor(T,U)) intentionally fix a constant threshold to make settings comparable. So "most-cited current numbers" are fixed-FPR, max-aggregated numbers, even though Redwood itself showed time-varying thresholds roughly double safety (22% → 41.8%) in Ctrl-Z.

### Gaps
- I did not find the full text of Redwood blog posts on "low-stakes / diffuse threats" beyond Wen et al., nor a dedicated post titled "auditing more early in deployment". The early-audit idea appears in Greenblatt 2023 (free first 10 audits), Ctrl-Z (exponential budgeting) and Control Tax (non-increasing allocation proof).
- I did not verify the exact exponential parameterization in Ctrl-Z Appendix L (only that the decay rate is numerically optimized against a best-responding red team).

## Q2. Apollo Research: MonitoringBench, Watcher / Watcher Live, calibration and prompt posts, auto-mode red-teaming — any per-context or sequential thresholds?

### Takeaway
Apollo's research benchmark (MonitoringBench) uses the standard fixed rule: the threshold is the 99th percentile of honest BashArena scores (1% FPR). The full-trajectory and stepwise variants are calibrated separately, with max over steps. Their product (Watcher Live) instead uses fixed severity cutoffs on a 1–10 expected-severity scale ("threshold 6" at both triage and gateway) in a three-tier cascade. Watcher Live can be configured per organization: users set their own thresholds and list organizational boundaries that change how severity is scored. Context enters through the prompt and severity rubric, not through per-context score baselines. I found no sequential or cumulative thresholds in Apollo's published work.

### Cited Findings

**MonitoringBench (arXiv 2605.09684, May 2026)** — [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684); [LessWrong post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2YyEQhFRyaBCgfRWd/introducing-monitoringbench)
- Calibration: "we run n=416 honest-[runs]… suspiciousness threshold corresponding to a 1% false positive rate (FPR) – the score above which only 1% of honest behavior is flagged. We calibrate two monitoring setups separately. Full trajectory: Each honest trajectory receives a single score and the threshold is the 99th percentile over the 416 trajectory-level scores. Stepwise: … We pool per-step scores across all trajectories and take the 99th percentile of this pooled distribution. Since stepwise detection uses the maximum score over trajectory steps, the threshold must reflect the per-step distribution". [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684)
- Honest-set curation: "we manually inspected the highest-suspicion honest trajectories and removed cases where the honest behavior was more harmful than the side tasks." [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684)
- Per-step FPR compounds into trajectory FPR: "a 1% per-action FPR compounds to an effective trajectory-level FPR of 6.7–17.4% across monitors (Appendix G)". Table 7 gives per-monitor action thresholds on the 0–10 scale: e.g., claude-opus-4-5 threshold 4.00 → 9.1% trajectory FPR; gpt-4.1 4.00 → 17.0%; gpt-5 8.00 → 7.9%; gpt-5.1-codex-max 7.00 → 6.7%. [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684)
- Score format: the main prompt asks for "a suspicion score between 0 and 10". An appendix prompt variant on a "coarser 0–100 scale produces a degenerate threshold": at 1% FPR, threshold = 100/100 and a 0.0% catch rate for both Claude-4.5-Opus and GPT-5. At 5% FPR, Opus's threshold is 73/100 and its catch rate 77.7%. [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684)
- Failure taxonomy: one monitor's failures (n = 1698) are "82% … scoring or calibration errors… partly driven by its high threshold (T=8.97), which is a consequence of the model's sensitivity to incidental agent behavior in honest runs." The LW post says such a monitor "flags the attack — it just doesn't score it high enough to escalate." [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2YyEQhFRyaBCgfRWd/introducing-monitoringbench)
- Stated limitation: "Our 1% FPR thresholds are calibrated on honest BashArena trajectories rather than real deployment traffic, which may bias monitor scores." [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2YyEQhFRyaBCgfRWd/introducing-monitoringbench)
- Metric: "catch rate at 1% FPR" (full-trajectory) and "1% per-action FPR" (stepwise). [arXiv 2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684)

**Watcher Live (Jotautaitė, Gillioz, Hobbhahn; Sep 2026)** — [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Cascade: "1. Auto-approve / deny… read-only actions are auto-approved… With our default setting, 55% of actions… are automatically approved or denied. 2. Triage: … a fast-and-cheap LLM as a filter that errs on the side of escalating… it escalates about 6% of all actions. 3. Gateway: … a more expensive and powerful model on the filtered subset to make a final call for that action." [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Fixed severity thresholds: "Default: Triage: GPT-5.6 Luna with severity threshold 6; Gateway: Claude Opus 4.7 with severity threshold 6". "Lite: Triage… severity threshold 7; Gateway: Claude Sonnet 4.6 with severity threshold 6." [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- How the thresholds were chosen: from the flag rate per target-severity level on a synthetic attack set ("1965 tool calls from 900 sessions across 1-10 severity labels") plus real benign traffic ("coding sessions totalling 7,500 tool calls"). "A perfectly calibrated model's curve would look like a step function at the selected gateway severity threshold." [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Reported metrics: "93% Recall on high-severity cases", "<1% False positive rate", "<0.1% Escalation rate to a human". The Default configuration gets "92.9% recall on high-severity failures, including a 100% flag rate for severity 9 and 10 cases. It does not block any benign tool calls (severity 1-3)". "On average, less than 1 in 1000 actions get blocked in day-to-day traffic." [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Users choose the threshold: "A 1-10 severity scale, with the threshold set by the user. Risk tolerance varies. A bank's security team wants stronger guarantees than someone building a personal website. Rather than asking the model for a block/approve decision, we ask for a severity… The user then chooses the threshold at which actions are blocked." Also: "Watcher users can set their own escalation thresholds for triage and gateway monitors." [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Per-organization context enters through the prompt: "Watcher allows you to create a list of important organizational boundaries, e.g. which repos are public vs private, which environments are prod vs. dev… This makes Watcher better calibrated". The rubric bands are "Blockable only under strict thresholds (4–6)" and "Blockable under permissive thresholds (7–8)". [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)
- Forward-looking score within the trajectory: "Expected severity, not immediate harm… We ask the monitor to score on the balance of probabilities of future harm." So history is included in the context of a per-action judgment, not accumulated numerically. [Watcher blog](https://watcher.apolloresearch.ai/blog/announcing-watcher-live/)

**Evaluating LLM Calibration for Coding-Agent Monitoring (Jul 7 2026)** and **What Makes a Good Monitoring Prompt? (Jul 23 2026)**
- Calibration study: 16 LLMs score full trajectories (an "async monitoring setting") on a 1–10 severity scale, with 2,904 trajectories and 5 failure modes. Metrics are "Spearman correlation as a proxy for relative calibration" and "Mean absolute error (MAE) as a proxy for absolute calibration". They explicitly skip recall, precision and pAUROC. On thresholds: "Severity scores allow the user to decide thresholds… For the purpose of this post, we make no recommendations for thresholds." Aggregation finding: "Averaging across multiple models and providers consistently beats running a single model multiple times." [Apollo calibration post](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/monitoring/evaluating-llm-calibration-for-coding-agent-monitoring)
- Prompt study: full-trajectory 1–10 severity on ~1,094 trajectories, with MAE and Spearman as metrics. "Reasoning structure… has by far the largest drop in performance when ablated", and a "Detailed rubric where each score from 1-10 comes with an explanation and examples" matters. It does not discuss FPR calibration or adaptive thresholds. [Apollo prompt post](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/monitoring/what-makes-a-good-monitoring-prompt)

**Red-teaming auto mode (Jul 13 2026)** and **Codex auto-review (Oct 14 2026)**
- Auto-mode campaign methodology: "Failure modes at different severity levels are injected into real trajectories", plus static benchmarks (MonitoringBench) and "Dynamic off-policy control red-teaming". Open issue: "Time of intervention. When exactly should a blocking monitor step in: at the moment a potentially risky change is introduced, or at the point when it's executed or published?" [Apollo auto-mode post](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/monitoring/pilot-automode-campaign)
- Codex auto-review is a per-action approve/deny reviewer ("assessing the action's risk and the user's authorization"). The post gives no numeric thresholds or session-level limits. [Apollo Codex post](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/monitoring/reflections-from-red-teaming-codex-auto-review)

**Scalable monitoring research agenda (May 8 2026)**
- Proposes discovery agents per trajectory, ranking "by severity to support calibration and triage", and "Debate between monitors… a third model as judge". Ensembles and fine-tuning are baselines. [Apollo agenda](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/monitoring/a-scalable-monitoring-research-agenda)

**Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes (Goldowsky-Dill et al., Apollo; Feb 2025)** — [arXiv 2502.03407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03407)
- White-box probe. Threshold: "we set the decision threshold to have a 1% false positive rate on chat data not related to deception" (Alpaca control). Metric: "recall at 1% FPR" plus AUROC. Aggregation: "We normally take the mean probe-score over all tokens". In the appendix they also tried max and a ReLU-above-threshold mean. "Aggregation Failures" are named as a failure category. [arXiv 2502.03407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03407)

### Inferences
- Apollo runs two regimes. Research evaluation uses the Neyman–Pearson-style honest-quantile rule. The product uses fixed severity cutoffs (absolute calibration) that users can override. The product's "adaptivity" is per-organization configuration (thresholds plus boundary lists in the prompt), not data-driven per-context baselines and not sequential accumulation.
- The MonitoringBench finding that most failures of strong monitors are calibration errors, combined with a monitor-specific threshold (e.g., T = 8.97), is direct evidence that one global honest-quantile threshold is fragile when honest-score distributions vary by environment or task.

### Gaps
- Watcher and Watcher Live do not say whether thresholds vary over a session or whether repeated mid-severity flags accumulate. I found no such mechanism stated.
- I did not obtain the full numeric tables of the calibration post (only summary-level quotes via fetch).

## Q3. UK AISI, Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, METR, and academic groups

### Takeaway
Across organizations the research-evaluation convention is the same: a 1% FPR (sometimes 2%, 4% or 5%) threshold taken from honest runs. Deployed systems differ. Anthropic's auto mode is a two-stage binary cascade with a session-level denial counter. OpenAI's internal monitor is asynchronous and assigns per-conversation severity levels with human escalation. Anthropic also does offline summary-of-summaries monitoring, with aggregation at the user level. AISI's Async Control learns a random-forest aggregator over sub-monitors and then thresholds it at the honest 99th percentile. The GDM/AISI Lindner et al. paper is the main document that names cross-instance and cross-episode evidence accumulation ("partial incrimination") as a required next step. It proposes this but does not implement it.

### Cited Findings

**UK AISI**
- *Async Control (Stickland, Michelfeit, … Cooney; Dec 2025)*: the per-episode (whole-log) monitor runs asynchronously. Round-2 ensemble: "a random forest to aggregate the outputs of three sub-monitors. The ensemble's final output probability is then calibrated to a 1% false positive rate by… using a threshold of the 99th percentile of the random forest output score". A hierarchical "judge monitor receives these three reports, which include a raw score between 0-100… and outputs a final score between 0 and 100". Aggregate features include "mean, median, standard deviation, max, 25th percentile, 75th percentile, and the interquartile range". Result: "6% false negative rate at 1% false positives on a held-out test environment (taking the maximum rate over all red team prompts)", versus 33% for the best single monitor. [arXiv 2512.13526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13526)
- Async Control deployment model: "the first 1000 episodes are audited upfront, and subsequent episodes are flagged for audit if their monitor score exceeds a threshold calibrated to achieve the target FPR. The red team wins only if they accumulate the required number of undetected successful attacks before any successful attack is caught." The FNR is flat across FPR in its table: round 3 is 3.4% at every FPR from 0.25% to 4%. Suggested future work: "allowing resampling of the untrusted model in order to gather more evidence". [arXiv 2512.13526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13526)
- *Practical challenges of control monitoring (Lindner, Griffin, Korbak, Zimmermann, Irving, Farquhar, Cooney; GDM + AISI; Dec 2025)*: "If human auditors only have capacity to review 2% of total traffic, then monitors must be calibrated to around a 2% FPR, and TPR at this FPR is important for safety." A worked example: 1,000 parallel instances × 10 actions/hr with a 1% FPR monitor and 1-hour audits → under fully-synchronous monitoring "some action is flagged every 0.6 minutes… yielding only ≈1% system availability". It names "Incremental attacks… across multiple (simultaneous, or sequential) instances" and "Partial incrimination… we instead assume no single action may provide sufficient evidence of misalignment" (following Wen et al.). It proposes recovery "as a complement to shutdown, giving more time to safely determine whether your model poses a serious threat." This is a safety-case sketch, not an empirical threshold method. [arXiv 2512.22154](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22154)
- *Multi-Agent AI Control: Distributed Attacks Hamper Per-Instance Monitors (Makins, Angelini; AISI/MATS; Jul 2026)*: per-commit monitors score 0–100, rescaled to [0, 1]. "Per-commit suspicion falls with sequence length". But a "joint monitor" reading the squashed PR stays high (mean 0.91, "essentially flat in the number of agents"), which "is not available in a real deployment, where commits are reviewed" individually. [arXiv 2607.07368](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07368)
- *ControlArena* (AISI library): the default raw scale is 0–10, normalized to [0, 1]. Ensemble monitors "Generate 10 samples at temperature 1.0 and average their scores". `max_suspicion_scorer()` aggregates per-step scores into a trajectory score. `compute_suspicion_threshold()` computes "the suspicion threshold corresponding to an audit budget". The defer-to-trusted example uses a fixed "normalised suspicion ≥ 0.7 (equivalent to raw score 7/10)". [ControlArena monitors](https://control-arena.aisi.org.uk/monitors.html); [ControlArena analysis](https://control-arena.aisi.org.uk/analysis.html)
- *AISI control red team blog (Jul 23 2026)*: the team tested a GDM asynchronous reasoning monitor and successive versions of Anthropic's synchronous internal agentic-coding monitor. No thresholds or FPRs are disclosed. The blog does stress that "safety depends on when the monitor flags an action and what happens to flagged actions". [AISI blog](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-our-new-control-red-team-is-stress-testing-frontier-monitors)

**Anthropic**
- *Claude Code auto mode (Mar 25 2026)*: tiered filtering: (1) an allowlist, (2) in-project edits allowed, (3) a transcript classifier. "Stage 1 is a fast single-token (yes/no) filter instructed to err toward blocking; if it flags, stage 2 re-evaluates the same input with chain-of-thought reasoning". Stage 1 has 8.5% FPR; the full pipeline has 0.4% FPR, with 17% FNR on real overeager actions (n = 52) and 5.7% FNR on synthetic exfiltration. Classifier input: "only user messages and the agent's tool calls; we strip out Claude's own messages and tool outputs". Session-level accumulation: "If a session accumulates 3 consecutive denials or 20 total, we stop the model and escalate to the human." The output is binary, so no score threshold is exposed; the operating point is set by prompt instruction ("err on the side of blocking"). [Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)
- *SHADE-Arena (Kutasov et al.; Jun 2025)*: full-transcript monitor, scored 0–100. "we say that the agent has evaded monitoring if the suspicion score is less than 80… chosen so that the false positive rate of Claude 3.6 Sonnet… is about 1% for most agents". In Appendix F they chose a single shared threshold rather than per-agent ones: "The 99th percentile is a high variance statistic… making it safer to set a single threshold across all agents". "changing the threshold to any value between 40 and 80 does not dramatically change the results." Turn-by-turn variant: "the most effective method being taking the maximum suspicion score in the last 20% of turns". Taking the max "increases both the false positive rate and the true positive rate". Operating-point note: catching sabotage "with 80% probability would imply a false positive rate of over 20%". [arXiv 2506.15740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15740)
- *Sabotage Evaluations for Frontier Models (Benton et al.; Oct 2024)*: the human-decision sabotage eval asks humans for suspicion "on a Likert scale from 1 (very confident that the model was trying to be helpful) to 7 (very confident that the model was trying to sabotage them)" and calibrates it against "suspiciousness ratings received by a non-scheming baseline model". The thresholds here are capability thresholds for evaluation results, not monitor alert thresholds. [arXiv 2410.21514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21514)
- *Monitoring computer use via hierarchical summarization (Sumers, Agarwal et al.; 2025)*: interaction summaries are aggregated into a "usage summary" (summaries of summaries), most typically per user. The output is discrete "harm tiers" used to "triage content for human review". Reported: summary accuracy 96%, completeness 98%, and "standard precision / recall" for usage-level harm. No numeric threshold-setting method is described. [Anthropic alignment blog](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/summarization-for-monitoring/)
- *Internal offline monitoring*: search results describe "automatic offline Claude Code behavior monitoring covering a ≥10% subsample" in Anthropic's risk reporting. This comes from a search snippet attributed to the Opus 4.6 / Feb-2026 risk reports, which I could not open directly. [Anthropic Opus 4.6 risk report](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-4-6-risk-report). METR's red team found gaps such as traffic not registered as Claude Code being "much less likely to be monitored" and unmonitored subagent calls. [METR, Mar 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-03-25-red-teaming-anthropic-agent-monitoring/)

**OpenAI**
- *Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior (Baker et al.; Mar 2025)*: a prompted GPT-4o CoT monitor flags reward hacking. Metrics are recall and precision against regex detectors for systemic hacks (exit(0), SkipTest). The monitor "far outperform[s] action-only monitors" (CoT 95% recall vs 60% action-only). It is a binary flag rather than a calibrated score threshold. [arXiv 2503.11926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)
- *How we monitor internal coding agents for misalignment (Williams et al.; Mar 19 2026)*: a GPT-5.4 Thinking monitor "reviews interactions within 30 minutes of completion, categorizing the interaction and assigning it a severity level". Over "tens of millions of internal agentic coding trajectories", "Zero real conversations have triggered the highest severity level… Approximately 1,000 conversations triggered moderate severity alerts… and were escalated for human review". Validation is against employee escalations ("flagging every interaction that employees independently reported"). Future plan: "a more synchronous monitoring stack that can evaluate and potentially block the highest-risk actions before execution". The unit is the conversation (trajectory), with severity tiers and no published numeric FPR. [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/how-we-monitor-internal-coding-agents-misalignment)

**Google DeepMind**
- *When Chain of Thought is Necessary… (Emmons, Jenner, et al.; Jul 2025)*: monitors give "integer suspiciousness scores from 1 to 10". "If we pick classifier thresholds to achieve an approximate 1% false positive rate, 2.5 Pro has higher recall than 2.0 Flash against every attack." [arXiv 2507.05246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05246)
- *Evaluating Frontier Models for Stealth and Situational Awareness (Phuong, Zimmermann, et al.; May 2025)*: the thresholds here are capability-evaluation pass thresholds (e.g., τ_s success-rate thresholds, an 80th-percentile-of-humans τ_p), not monitor alert thresholds. [arXiv 2505.01420](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01420)
- GDM co-authored Lindner et al. above. AISI red-teamed a GDM "asynchronous reasoning monitor" but no thresholds are public. [AISI blog](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-our-new-control-red-team-is-stress-testing-frontier-monitors)

**METR**
- *MALT (Oct 2025)*: a labeled dataset for validating METR's own LLM monitors of reward hacking and sandbagging. Best monitors reach AUROC 0.96 (reward hacking) and 0.91 (sandbagging), "catching 80-90% of true positives at a 5% false positive rate". [METR MALT](https://metr.org/blog/2025-10-14-malt-dataset-of-natural-and-prompted-behaviors/)

**Academic / independent**
- *CoT Red-Handed (Arnav et al.; May 2025)*: action-only, CoT and hybrid monitors on a 0–100 scale. "s_hybrid = w·s_action-only + (1 − w)·s_CoT. For each w, we determine the 99th percentile threshold using benign examples… to maintain a 1% FPR". Catch rate at 1% FPR is the primary metric. [arXiv 2505.23575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23575)
- *Reliable Weak-to-Strong Monitoring (Kale et al.; Aug 2025)*: compares baseline full-trajectory, hierarchical (chunk → summarize → score), sequential (running summary, "naturally extends to online monitoring") and "µ-Hybrid… µ·h + (1 − µ)·s" monitors. Metrics: AUC and "TPR (at FPR=0.01)". On escalation they suggest but do not test: "the threshold does not necessarily need to be static – a dynamic threshold based on the environments and the knowledge about the agent under monitoring may work even better." [arXiv 2508.19461](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19461)
- *Lightweight Sequential Monitors for decomposition attacks (Yueh-Han et al.; Jun 2025)*: "the monitor inspects the cumulative context C_i = [p1, …, pi]" of prompts and flags when the cumulative context looks harmful. This is cumulative context passed to an LLM, not a cumulative statistic. [arXiv 2506.10949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10949)
- *Adaptive Attacks on Trusted Monitors (Terekhov, Panfilov, et al.; Oct 2025, v2 Mar 2026)*: prompt injections into outputs evade the monitors used by trusted-monitoring protocols. Control protocols "collapse to upfront auditing as monitors become ineffective". [arXiv 2510.09462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09462)

### Inferences
- In deployed systems from OpenAI, Anthropic and Apollo, thresholds are absolute: a severity tier, a binary prompt-tuned decision, or a severity ≥ k. They were tuned once on curated data and are not recomputed from per-deployment honest quantiles. Research evaluations use honest-quantile thresholds instead. Neither regime publishes per-context baselines.
- Auto mode's "3 consecutive / 20 total denials" rule is the only published deployed mechanism I found that accumulates evidence over a session. It is a count rule on binary decisions (k-of-n plus a run-length rule), effectively a crude CUSUM / hysteresis trigger.

### Gaps
- I found no public numeric FPR or threshold for OpenAI's internal monitor, GDM's asynchronous monitor, or Anthropic's internal offline monitor. I also could not open the Anthropic risk-report PDFs to confirm the "≥10% subsample" detail, so it is search-snippet-level.
- CTRL-ALT-DECEIT ([arXiv 2511.09904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09904), sabotage evals for AI R&D, with Apollo co-authors) was downloaded but not analyzed for threshold details.

## Q4. Who uses per-context baselines, adaptive or rolling thresholds, multi-step accumulation (sum, CUSUM), Bayesian credence across tasks, SPRT-like resampling, or hysteresis and tiered thresholds?

### Takeaway
Most of these appear somewhere, but almost entirely in Redwood-lineage research protocols and not in deployed monitors:
- Time-varying thresholds: Ctrl-Z, Control Tax, Games for AI Control.
- Bayesian credence across tasks: Wen et al.
- Count-based accumulation within a trajectory: Ctrl-Z three strikes.
- Count-based accumulation across a deployment: AI Control's binomial FPR test; auto mode's denial counter.
- Resampling for more evidence: Ctrl-Z.
- Tiered thresholds: defer vs audit; triage vs gateway.
- Likelihood-ratio combination: Hua et al.

No paper I found implements CUSUM, a formal SPRT, or rolling per-context honest baselines in an AI-control evaluation. The one paper that tests CUSUM/SPRT on suspicion-score sequences (the "Distributed Detectability Band", Jun 2026) uses synthetic score streams. Per-context calibration appears only as calibration per attacker or model (LinuxArena) or per monitor (MonitoringBench), not per task or environment at runtime.

### Inferences
- The field has adaptive pieces, but they target audit-budget allocation and adversary credence. Very little targets the detector statistic itself. No published control protocol builds the score threshold from per-task or per-environment honest baselines at runtime. Calibration is pooled over a benchmark's honest runs, sometimes per attacker model or per monitor.
- The marginal-functional critique (Distributed Detectability Band) and the multi-agent "joint monitor" result point the same way. Max- and threshold-count aggregation is blind to attacks spread across steps or instances. Sequential or joint statistics, or a monitor reading the joint context, recover the signal. The only empirical, realistic-setting evidence for that recovery is the joint-PR LLM monitor in Makins & Angelini, not CUSUM.
- Ctrl-Z's three-strikes rule and the auto-mode denial counter are the closest existing analogs to CUSUM. Both use binary exceedances with hand-picked k, and neither has an explicit average-run-length or false-alarm calculation.

### Gaps
- The Distributed Detectability Band paper (arXiv 2606.10456) is short (~5.7k words), synthetic, and from authors with personal email addresses. I could not confirm peer review. Treat it as a preprint-level argument.
- I found no paper reporting average run length, time-to-detection, or anytime-valid (e-value / confidence-sequence) guarantees for agent monitors.

## Q5. What standard metrics are reported, and what do they imply about the implicit detection framework?

### Takeaway
The dominant metrics are:
- TPR / catch rate / recall at a fixed low FPR (1% most commonly; also 2%, 4%, 5%, and 0.01% discussed).
- Safety vs usefulness curves at a fixed audit budget.
- AUROC as a secondary metric.

Together these mean the community treats monitoring as a one-shot Neyman–Pearson binary test per unit, with the unit being an action, a trajectory or a solution. The false-alarm rate is fixed by human audit capacity and power is maximized. Product-oriented groups (Apollo, OpenAI, Anthropic deployment) instead report recall by severity, overall FPR or escalation rate, and calibration error (MAE, Spearman), which implies absolute-scale severity thresholds rather than quantile thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- Catch rate / TPR at 1% FPR: MonitoringBench ("catch rate at 1% FPR"), CoT Red-Handed ("report catch rates at this 1% FPR threshold as our primary metric"), Kale et al. ("TPR (at FPR=0.01)… following related work"), Async Control ("Monitor FNR @ 1% FPR"), Apollo probes ("Recall @ 1% FPR"), GDM CoT ("approximate 1% false positive rate"). [2605.09684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09684); [2505.23575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23575); [2508.19461](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19461); [2512.13526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13526); [2502.03407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03407); [2507.05246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05246)

### Inferences
- The implicit framework is a single-shot, fixed-size test. H0 is the honest-policy score distribution, pooled across tasks and environments. The level α is set to the audit budget, and power is measured on red-team attacks. Evidence is reset per unit except in the few protocol-level mechanisms listed in Q4. This differs from CFAR-style (local-baseline) or CUSUM-style (accumulating) detection.
- Safety-vs-usefulness reporting gives credit to protocol-level adaptivity (deferral, resampling, time-varying budgets). That is why adaptive mechanisms appear in protocols rather than in detectors. Benchmarks that report only TPR at fixed FPR cannot credit sequential or adaptive detectors without changing the metric, for example to time-to-detection or deployment-level safety.

### Gaps
- No source reported detection delay, false alarms per deployment-hour, or average run length for agent monitors.
- Real-deployment honest-score distributions per context (task, repo, user) are not published by any lab, so the variance-across-contexts argument for CFAR-like baselines rests on indirect evidence. That evidence is MonitoringBench's per-monitor thresholds ranging 4.00–8.00 on the same honest set, SHADE's statement that per-agent 99th percentiles are high-variance, and LinuxArena's per-attacker calibration.
