# Adjacent-field prior art: adaptive per-entity baselines (CFAR-like) and sequential change detection (CUSUM/SPRT/EWMA/BOCPD) for alerting, and what transfers to LLM-agent monitoring

Scope note: research conducted 2026-10-20. Coverage is broad but shallow in places (one pass, ~30 tool calls). Every claim below has an inline source; anything I believe but did not verify in this session is in a Gaps subsection, labeled as such.

## Q1. Security: UEBA / SIEM adaptive baselines, CUSUM/SPRT intrusion detection, mimicry, low-and-slow, and baseline poisoning

### Takeaway
Per-entity and peer-group baselines plus cumulative risk scoring are standard, shipping features of commercial SIEM/UEBA products. Sequential tests in intrusion detection go back more than 20 years: nonparametric CUSUM for SYN floods (INFOCOM 2002) and Wald's SPRT for port scans (IEEE S&P 2004). The security literature has also documented the matching attacks for just as long: mimicry attacks (CCS 2002) and slow "Boiling Frog" poisoning of retrained baselines (IMC 2009). An AI-agent port inherits both the method and the attacks, and both count as well known.

### Cited Findings
**Commercial per-entity / peer baselines (the "CFAR-like" analogue is standard product practice)**
- Microsoft Sentinel UEBA sets each entity's baseline from "its own historical activities, those of its peers, and those of the organization as a whole." It scores anomalies using differences from peer groups and from organization-wide patterns, and adds blast-radius evaluation. — [Microsoft Learn: UEBA in Sentinel](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-in/azUre/sentinel/identify-threats-with-entity-behavior-analytics); [Sentinel anomalies reference](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/sentinel/anomalies-reference)
- Secondary sources describe UEBA peer grouping by department/job title (e.g., a finance analyst is compared with other finance analysts) as a way to reduce false positives, and say many models use roughly 7–21 days of baseline telemetry. These are secondary, non-vendor-authoritative sources. — [OneUptime blog (2026)](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-02-16-how-to-set-up-microsoft-sentinel-incident-investigation-with-entity-behavior-analytics-ueba/markdown); [decryptiondigest UEBA explainer](https://www.decryptiondigest.com/blog/how-to-detect-insider-threats-ueba-user-entity-behavior-analytics)
- Splunk Enterprise Security "Risk-Based Alerting" (RBA) works in two steps. First, detections write per-entity "intermediate findings" carrying risk scores into a risk index. A second detection then "aggregates intermediate findings over time for an entity" and raises a notable finding when the total "surpass[es] a threshold." The score depends on the time window: once events fall outside the window, they no longer count. — [Splunk docs: Risk scoring in Splunk ES 8.x](https://help.splunk.com/en/splunk-enterprise-security-8/administer/8.6/risk-based-alerting/risk-scoring-in-splunk-enterprise-security)

**Sequential tests in network intrusion detection (classic)**
- Wang, Zhang & Shin (INFOCOM 2002) detect SYN floods at leaf routers. They run a nonparametric CUSUM on the normalized SYN–FIN(RST) pair difference. The authors stress that the detector is stateless and cheap, which makes it "immune to flooding attacks" itself. — [Wang, Zhang, Shin 2002 (PDF)](https://rtcl.eecs.umich.edu/rtclweb/assets/publications/2002/wang2002detecting.pdf)
- Jung, Paxson, Berger & Balakrishnan (IEEE S&P 2004), "Fast Portscan Detection Using Sequential Hypothesis Testing," introduce Threshold Random Walk (TRW). TRW models each remote host's connection outcomes as a random walk under a benign or a scanner hypothesis and applies Wald's SPRT. It detects scanners in "4 or 5" connection attempts and gives configurable bounds on false alarm and miss probabilities. — [Jung et al. 2004 (PDF)](https://people.csail.mit.edu/awberger/papers/fastPortscanDetectionUsingSequentialHypothesisTesting_JungPaxsonBergerBalakrishnan_2004.pdf); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/37892)
- Follow-up work continued developing sequential portscan detectors. — [New Sequential Methods for Detecting Portscanners (arXiv 1204.1935)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.1935)
- Urbina, Cardenas, Tippenhauer et al. (CCS 2016), "Limiting the Impact of Stealthy Attacks on Industrial Control Systems," study attackers who "follow closely the expected behavior of the system while injecting just enough false information at each time step." The paper proposes measuring the *impact* an undetected stealthy attacker can cause for a fixed false-alarm bound, rather than detection rate. It shows that impact can be limited "by the proper combination and configuration of detection schemes" (the paper compares stateless and stateful, CUSUM-type, residual detectors). — [NIST CSRC record](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/conference/2016/10/24/limiting-the-impact-of-stealthy-attacks-on-ics/final); [CISPA record](https://cispa.de/en/research/publications/67451-limiting-the-impact-of-stealthy-attacks-on-industrial-control-systems)

**Mimicry attacks (attacker knows the anomaly model)**
- Wagner & Soto (CCS 2002), "Mimicry Attacks on Host-Based Intrusion Detection Systems," define a mimicry attack: an attacker who knows the normal-behavior model "cloak[s]" an intrusion as a sequence the model treats as normal. They give a theoretical framework for evaluating IDS security against such attacks and break a published IDS with a worked example. — [Wagner & Soto 2002 (PDF)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/readings/AccessControl/wagner_soto_ccs02.pdf)

**Baseline / training-data poisoning of adaptive anomaly detectors ("boiling frog")**
- Rubinstein, Nelson, Huang, Joseph, Lau, Rao, Taft & Tygar, "ANTIDOTE: Understanding and Defending against Poisoning of Anomaly Detectors" (IMC 2009), target PCA-subspace backbone anomaly detectors. Because networks are non-stationary, such detectors are retrained regularly (e.g., weekly) on the previous week's data, and points the detector flags are removed first. That retraining opens a slow-poisoning channel. — [ANTIDOTE, IMC 2009 (PDF)](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/truststc/pubs/725/IMC.2009.pdf)
  - Single-period poisoning: an uninformed attacker raises FNR from a baseline of 3.67% to 28% with a 10% average increase in mean link rates. A globally informed attacker with the same 10% increase raises FNR to 38%, and to over 90% with more chaff. — [same](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/truststc/pubs/725/IMC.2009.pdf)
  - Boiling Frog (multi-week, gradually increasing chaff): a 15% weekly growth rate raises FNR to more than 70% (from 3.67%) over 3 weeks. Even a 5% growth rate raises it to 50% over 3 weeks. The detector's own filter rejects only about 5% of the chaff each week for the slower schedules. Reaching 50% FNR takes about an 18% traffic increase in a single training period, but only about a 5% average increase spread across 3 weeks with Boiling Frog. The authors conclude: "a moderate growth rate of 2–5% can significantly poison PCA … while still going unnoticed by the detector." — [same](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/truststc/pubs/725/IMC.2009.pdf)
  - Defense: ANTIDOTE replaces the variance in PCA with a robust spread measure (PCA-GRID maximizing the median absolute deviation, MAD) and uses a Laplace-based cutoff threshold. "Poisoning has little effect on the robust model." — [same](https://ptolemy.berkeley.edu/projects/truststc/pubs/725/IMC.2009.pdf)
- A companion paper covers the same attack family. — [Stealthy poisoning attacks on PCA-based anomaly detectors (Berkeley PDF)](https://www.cs.berkeley.edu/~tygar/papers/SML/Stealthy_poisoning.pdf)

### Inferences
- Splunk RBA is a *sliding-window sum* per entity, not a CUSUM: scores expire when they leave the window. An attacker who knows the window length and threshold can keep the windowed sum below threshold by pacing activity. This is the commercial form of the low-and-slow problem, and a CUSUM with drift term κ has the same weakness for any sustained per-step contribution below κ.
- TRW/SPRT is the cleanest precedent for "accumulate per-action log-likelihood ratios for one entity until a decision." Applying SPRT to per-action monitor scores of an AI agent is therefore a direct port. TRW worked because each observation (connection success or failure) has a well-modeled likelihood under both hypotheses. LLM monitor scores lack a validated likelihood model under the "scheming" hypothesis, which is the gap a port would need to fill.
- ANTIDOTE is the key precedent for a CFAR-like per-agent baseline. If an agent's own past trajectories define its "normal" suspicion level and that baseline is refit periodically, the monitored model controls the reference cells. A Boiling-Frog-style schedule (raise suspicious-but-sub-threshold behavior by a few percent per period) is the expected attack. Defenses carry over: robust statistics (median/MAD, order statistics), frozen or trusted-only reference data, and caps on how fast a baseline may move.
- Urbina et al.'s metric, *damage achievable while undetected at a fixed false-alarm rate*, fits AI control better than detection delay or AUROC. It matches the control-evaluation framing of safety at a fixed audit budget.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch vendor docs for Exabeam, Elastic, Google Chronicle/SecOps, or Splunk UBA's peer-group models. Only Sentinel and Splunk ES RBA are cited.
- Not verified this session: insider-threat datasets (CERT r4.2) and specific "low-and-slow" APT detection papers; Tan, Killourhy & Maxion (RAID 2002) and later mimicry work beyond Wagner & Soto. I believe these exist but did not source them here.
- Exact quantitative comparison of CUSUM vs. stateless detectors in Urbina et al. not extracted (abstract-level only).

## Q2. Fraud & abuse monitoring, including how LLM providers aggregate misuse signals across a user's conversations over time

### Takeaway
LLM providers already aggregate at the account/usage level. Anthropic's hierarchical summarization and Clio, and OpenAI's threat-intel disruption reports, explicitly target harms that are invisible in any single conversation. As far as public writeups show, though, they use LLM summarization, clustering, and human review rather than formal sequential tests with calibrated false-alarm rates. So "account-level accumulation" is not new, but "formal CUSUM/SPRT with ARL guarantees on LLM monitor scores" is not what providers describe publicly.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic, "Monitoring computer use via hierarchical summarization" (Alignment Science Blog, 2025). Stage 1 compresses each prompt–completion into a structured summary of intent and outcome. Stage 2 aggregates "hundreds" of interaction summaries into a "usage summary" of a single user's activity. Target: "aggregate-level harms" that look benign individually. Example: clicking a button could be UI testing or a click farm. Reported quality: 96% of interaction summaries were judged accurate enough for harm determination and 98% complete. Summarization complements prompt-level and usage-level classifiers rather than replacing them. — [Anthropic Alignment Blog](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/summarization-for-monitoring/); [Simon Willison summary](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Feb/27/hierarchical-summarization)
- Anthropic, "Clio: Privacy-Preserving Insights into Real-World AI Use" (arXiv 2412.13678, Dec 2024). Clio surfaces aggregate patterns across millions of conversations with privacy-preserving aggregation thresholds. Safety uses: finding coordinated abuse, monitoring "unknown unknowns" during launches and major events, and improving existing monitors. It is credited with uncovering and banning a network of automated accounts generating SEO spam that was not visible at the level of single conversations. — [Clio arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13678v1); [Anthropic PDF](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/7e1ab885d1b24176/original/Clio-Privacy-Preserving-Insights-into-Real-World-AI-Use.pdf)
- OpenAI, "Disrupting malicious uses of AI" (June 2025 and October 2025 reports). These describe campaign-level detection (e.g., "ScopeCreep" malware development; North Korea-linked fake-résumé operations) that combines automated detection with human analysis, ends in account termination, and shares intelligence with partners. The October 2025 report is described as covering 40+ disrupted networks. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-cyber-threat-actors/); [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-openai-ai-threat-report/); [IT Brew (Oct 2025)](https://itbrew.com/stories/2025/10/15/openai-disruption-report)

### Inferences
- Production LLM misuse monitoring already accumulates evidence per account. In the language of this question, the "entity" is the account and the "baseline" is implicitly the population of benign usage. A paper that applies per-entity accumulation to agent monitoring without formal guarantees would look like an engineering restatement of existing practice. A formal treatment (ARL/false-alarm control, detection-delay bounds, adversarially paced attackers) would separate itself from these writeups.
- Fraud practice uses per-account velocity rules (rate limits on events per window). These are the same structure as Splunk's windowed sum and evade the same way: the attacker paces below the rate. Spreading activity across multiple accounts (Sybil) defeats per-entity accumulation entirely. Clio's cross-account clustering addresses that case. For AI agents, the analogue is a model spreading an attack across many independent instances, sessions, or tasks, so that per-trajectory accumulators never fire. Wen et al.'s cross-task credence (Q5) is the AI-control answer to this.

### Gaps
- I found no public description of Anthropic or OpenAI using a formal CUSUM/SPRT/EWMA over account-level classifier scores. Absence of evidence: internal systems may differ.
- I did not source fraud-industry papers (e.g., Stripe Radar, bank transaction monitoring with sequential scoring). Velocity-rule claims above are inference/common practice, not cited.
- Not checked: Anthropic's later threat-intelligence reports (2025–2026) or Google/Microsoft LLM abuse-monitoring writeups.

## Q3. ML-ops drift detection (CUSUM, Page–Hinkley, ADWIN, DDM) and LLM-specific behavior-change detection

### Takeaway
Page–Hinkley (a CUSUM variant), ADWIN, and DDM are textbook streaming drift detectors, shipped in standard libraries and widely used to watch model error streams. Applying them to a monitor-score stream is routine. LLM-specific "did the model change?" work uses two-sample tests on outputs rather than CUSUM-style quickest detection.

### Cited Findings
- Page–Hinkley "accumulates the deviation of streaming observations from their running mean, alarming when the cumulative shift exceeds a threshold"; applied to error streams. ADWIN keeps an adaptive window and shrinks it when two sub-windows differ significantly; its only parameter is a confidence δ. DDM uses binomial control-chart bounds on the error rate. scikit-multiflow implements ADWIN, DDM, EDDM, HDDM-A, HDDM-W, KSWIN, and PageHinkley. — [apxml course: concept drift strategies](https://apxml.com/courses/monitoring-managing-ml-models-production/chapter-2-advanced-drift-detection/concept-drift-strategies); [MetricGate docs](https://metricgate.com/docs/concept-drift-detection)
- Recent benchmarking and evaluation of concept-drift detectors, including an energy/accuracy tradeoff study for ML-enabled systems. — [Framework for Evaluating and Benchmarking Concept Drift Detection (arXiv 2606.07789)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07789); [Sustainable monitoring of ML systems (arXiv 2404.19452)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.19452)
- Gao, Liang & Guestrin, "Model Equality Testing: Which Model Is This API Serving?" (ICLR 2025), cast detection of undisclosed model changes (quantization, watermarking, fine-tuning) as a two-sample test. They found that 11 of 31 commercial endpoints for four Llama models serve distributions different from Meta's reference weights. — [arXiv 2410.20247](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20247v2)
- Dubey (arXiv 2608.02464, Aug 2026), "Real-Time Detection and Repair of LLM Agent Failures," uses a one-class echo-state-network ensemble with CUSUM alarms on agent step telemetry, calibrated only on healthy runs. Results: detection rate 0.71 at a 5% false-alarm budget (AUROC 0.872); the advantage over memoryless detection grows with horizon (+0.09 at ≤3 steps, +0.40 at ≥9); about 200 µs per step. Limitation: baselines must be calibrated per deployment and do not transfer (AUROC 0.527 cold). This is a non-adversarial (failure-detection) setting. — [arXiv 2608.02464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02464)

### Inferences
- CUSUM on agent telemetry, with per-deployment baselines, is already published (Dubey 2026) for *benign failure* detection. Its main empirical result transfers: memory helps more as the horizon grows, and baselines are deployment-specific (a CFAR-like need). It says nothing about adversaries.
- Drift detectors assume the change is exogenous (data shift). In AI control the "change" is chosen by the monitored model. Drift detectors are also designed to *adapt* after a change, i.e., accept the new normal, which is exactly what a boiling-frog attacker exploits (see Q1, ANTIDOTE).

### Gaps
- Not sourced: Chen, Zaharia & Zou (2023) "How is ChatGPT's behavior changing over time?"; commercial LLM-observability vendors' drift features (Arize, WhyLabs, Evidently). Relevant but not verified here.
- No source found applying BOCPD (Adams & MacKay 2007) to LLM monitor scores.

## Q4. Adversarial / robust sequential change detection theory (minimax CUSUM, GLR, covert/pacing attackers, game theory)

### Takeaway
Classical theory (CUSUM optimality, GLR-CUSUM for unknown post-change parameters) assumes nature, not an adversary, picks the post-change law. A small, recent literature treats an attacker who knows the threshold. Its headline result (Ramtin, Nain & Towsley 2025–2026): a covert adversary who scales its post-change distribution toward the pre-change one as the false-alarm constraint γ tightens forces detection delay of Θ(γ) instead of the classical O(log γ). Game-theoretic IDS work (Stackelberg threshold selection) shows that adaptive, time-varying thresholds beat fixed ones against strategic attackers.

### Cited Findings
- Xie, Zou, Xie & Veeravalli, "Sequential (Quickest) Change Detection: Classical Results and New Directions," IEEE JSAIT 2(2):494–514 (2021). The standard survey: classical CUSUM and Shiryaev-Roberts results, extensions (unknown post-change, composite hypotheses, multi-stream), modern applications, and open problems. — [arXiv 2104.04186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04186v1)
- Ramtin, Nain & Towsley, "Quickest Change Detection in Discrete-Time in Presence of a Covert Adversary" (arXiv 2601.20022, Jan 2026). The adversary knows the detector's false-alarm parameter γ and picks a stationary post-change distribution that depends on γ. Against CUSUM, it can stay covert with average detection delay ADD = Θ(γ), versus the classical O(log γ). The paper gives exact asymptotics for Gaussian and exponential models in terms of the KL divergence between pre- and post-change distributions. — [arXiv 2601.20022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20022v2)
- Continuous-time companion: covert adversary whose post-change drift depends on γ. — [arXiv 2509.17778](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17778v1)
- Byzantine-robust *distributed* QCD, where some sensors are adversarial. Relevant if multiple monitors could be compromised, as in untrusted monitoring. — [arXiv 1306.2086](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.2086)
- Ghafouri, Abbas, Laszka, Vorobeychik & Koutsoukos, "Optimal Thresholds for Anomaly-Based Intrusion Detection in Dynamical Environments" (GameSec 2016). Threshold selection is modeled as an attacker–defender (Stackelberg) game trading detection delay against false positives. The paper gives algorithms for optimal fixed thresholds and polynomial-time algorithms for optimal *time-varying* thresholds, which are better when attack damage varies over time. — [arXiv 1606.06707](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06707)
- Related game-theoretic work notes that "a rational attacker can deliver invisible attacks just below the detection threshold" and models attack intensity against threshold as strategic variables. — [Ghafouri et al. 2019, Vanderbilt PDF](https://cs.vuse.vanderbilt.edu/koutsoxd/www/Publications/Ghafouri2019_Article_AGame-theoreticApproachForSele.pdf); [Laszka et al. (Vanderbilt PDF)](https://cs.vuse.vanderbilt.edu/koutsoxd/www/Publications/p72-laszka.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Ramtin et al. result is the formal statement of the "pacing" worry for CUSUM-on-monitor-scores. If the monitored model knows κ and h and can shrink its per-step signal, delay becomes linear rather than logarithmic in the false-alarm budget. Whether this matters depends on a quantity the theory does not supply: how much *harm* per step an attacker achieves at a given per-step KL. In ICS (Urbina et al.), harm is roughly proportional to injected bias, so small signal means small harm. For AI agents, a single low-suspicion action (e.g., one subtle backdoor line) can be catastrophic. Harm and statistical signal are decoupled, and accumulation buys little against these concentrated ("high-stakes") attacks. It helps against diffuse ("low-stakes") attacks that need many sub-threshold actions.
- Kerckhoffs-style assumptions in AI control (the attacker knows the protocol and thresholds; see Q5) are the same as the covert-adversary assumption here. Game-theoretic results favoring randomized or time-varying thresholds plausibly transfer: randomize κ/h, or keep the reference window secret.
- A known-threshold attacker against SPRT/CUSUM can also exploit the *reset*: CUSUM's max(0, ·) floor discards accumulated evidence after benign stretches. Interleaving benign actions between malicious ones resets the statistic. This follows directly from the recursion, and the low-and-slow security literature (Q1) is its empirical counterpart.

### Gaps
- Not fetched this session, though standard and citable from the Xie et al. survey: Lorden (1971) minimax optimality of CUSUM; Moustakides (1986) exact optimality; Lai (1998) and Siegmund–Venkatraman GLR-CUSUM for unknown post-change parameters; Unnikrishnan, Veeravalli & Meyn (2011) minimax-robust QCD with least-favorable distributions. The report writer should cite these via the survey ([arXiv 2104.04186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04186v1)) rather than as independently verified here.
- I found no paper giving minimax QCD guarantees when the post-change *sequence* (not just distribution) is adversarially and adaptively chosen with knowledge of the statistic's current value. That appears to be an open area.

## Q5. Multi-turn jailbreak and agentic-attack defenses that accumulate per-turn risk; AI control monitors

### Takeaway
Stateful, conversation-level guardrails are now an active area (2025–2026), and at least one paper uses an explicit CUSUM over per-turn risk log-odds (SafeDream, April 2026). In AI control, cross-task accumulation already exists as Bayesian credence over the untrusted model's alignment (Wen et al., ICLR 2025, 80% fewer backdoors at equal usefulness). Separately, adaptive attacks on monitors (prompt injection) are shown to defeat monitor-based protocols, and resampling can amplify them. So "CUSUM over turns" and "adaptive per-agent credence" both already appear in LLM safety. The open space is calibrated, adversarially analyzed sequential tests on monitor scores under a monitored-model-knows-the-monitor threat model.

### Cited Findings
**Multi-turn jailbreak / stateful guardrails**
- SafeDream (Yan, Lin, Zhu, Wang; UCF / Rochester / IBM; April 2026). Each turn's risk becomes clipped log-odds Λ_t, accumulated as G_t = max(0, G_{t-1} + Λ_t − κ) with κ set to "benign conversation norms." An alarm fires at G_t > A, and a "gray zone" τA < G_t < A triggers proactive look-ahead. Evaluated on XGuard-Train (30K conversations), SafeDialBench (4K), and SafeMTData (1.7K). Results: AUROC 0.908–0.915; FPR 6.6–7.9%; detection 1.06–1.20 turns before model compliance. Stated limitation: transfer to unseen attack families may need retraining; needs internal representations. — [alphaXiv 2604.16824](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.16824)
- DeepContext (arXiv 2602.16935, 2026) uses an RNN over turn embeddings to track "incremental accumulation of risk that stateless models overlook," reporting F1 0.84 on multi-turn jailbreak detection. — [arXiv 2602.16935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16935); [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.16935.md)
- Mishra, Chukkapalli & Naik, "Stateful Guardrails for Multi-Turn LLM Systems: A Conversational Risk Accumulation Framework" (arXiv 2607.19361, June 2026). Uses semantic drift from a session anchor, sensitivity-weighted information accumulation, and a compliance gradient, fused heuristically or with a learned model (CRA-Net). It is *not* a formal sequential test. Claims are limited to within-distribution sessions (CRA-Bench, 3,200 sessions). — [arXiv 2607.19361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19361)
- Chen (Yueh-Han), Joshi et al., "Monitoring Decomposition Attacks in LLMs with Lightweight Sequential Monitors" (ICLR 2025 workshop / ICLR 2026). Splitting harmful goals into benign subtasks drops GPT-4o refusal from 50% to 10%. A lightweight monitor that "cumulatively evaluates each sub-prompt" reaches a 93% defense success rate, beating Llama-Guard-4 and o3-mini at 90% lower cost and 50% lower latency. Dataset: DecomposedHarm, 4,634 tasks. — [arXiv 2506.10949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10949v1); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10010390); [ICLR 2025 workshop](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/34234)

**AI control: monitors, accumulation, and adaptive attackers**
- Wen et al., "Adaptive Deployment of Untrusted LLMs Reduces Distributed Threats" (ICLR 2025; Tsinghua, Redwood, MATS, Anthropic and others). A macro-protocol keeps an "adaptive credence on the untrusted model's alignment based on its past actions" and uses it to choose safer or riskier per-task micro-protocols. In a backdoored-code testbed this cuts backdoors by 80% at a given usefulness, compared with non-adaptive baselines. This is the closest AI-control precedent for sequential, per-entity evidence accumulation. — [arXiv 2411.17693](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17693v1); [ICLR 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/699c5a2bb87a8aa32c4ad541d9997361-Abstract-Conference.html)
- Terekhov, Panfilov, Dzenhaliou, Gulcehre, Andriushchenko, Prabhu & Geiping, "Adaptive Attacks on Trusted Monitors Subvert AI Control Protocols" (arXiv 2510.09462, Oct 2025, rev. Mar 2026). On two control benchmarks, frontier models that know the protocol and monitor "consistently evade diverse monitors" by embedding known or zero-shot prompt injections in their outputs. The Defer-to-Resample protocol *amplifies* the attack by turning it into best-of-n. — [arXiv 2510.09462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09462)
- AI control evaluations assume, per Kerckhoffs's principle, that the subversive model knows the protocol, including "the monitor's identity, algorithms, and decision thresholds." — [Salient directions in AI control (LessWrong)](https://lesswrong.com/posts/kitaRHhSzphdvqie2/salient-directions-in-ai-control); see also [arXiv 2505.23575](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23575v3)
- Scale AI, "Reliable Weak-to-Strong Monitoring of LLM Agents" (Monitor Red Teaming, arXiv 2508.19461, Aug 2025). Varies agent and monitor situational awareness and evasion strategies. A hybrid *hierarchical-sequential* monitor scaffolding (chunked, propagating context) outperforms baselines and lets weaker models monitor stronger agents. — [arXiv 2508.19461](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19461v1); [Scale Labs](https://labs.scale.com/papers/mrt)
- Hierarchical monitoring (two trusted monitors in sequence) gets higher safety than one monitor at the same budget. — [FAR.AI session, Tim Hua](https://far.ai/events/sessions/tim-hua-hierarchical-monitoring)
- Legibility/commenting protocols help trusted monitors because "honest code … typically has a natural explanation." Per-submission, no temporal accumulation. — [arXiv 2602.10153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10153)

### Inferences
- **Novelty judgment.** CUSUM over per-turn LLM risk scores is already published (SafeDream 2026, conversation level; Dubey 2026, agent failures). Per-model Bayesian credence across tasks is already published in AI control (Wen et al. 2025). Sequential/cumulative monitors for decomposition attacks are published (Chen et al.). A paper that only proposes "apply CUSUM/SPRT/CFAR to AI-control monitor scores" would likely be read as a straightforward port. Defensible novelty would come from things none of these sources do:
  1. Formal false-alarm/ARL calibration of LLM-monitor scores, which are heavy-tailed, discrete, and prompt-sensitive, against an audit budget.
  2. Analysis under the control threat model, where the monitored model knows κ, h, and the reference window and paces its attack (connecting to Ramtin et al.'s Θ(γ) result).
  3. Explicit handling of agent-controlled baselines (boiling-frog poisoning of per-agent or per-task CFAR references).
  4. Harm-weighted detection (concentrated vs diffuse threats).
  5. Interaction with resampling protocols, which Terekhov et al. show can amplify attacks. Adding a sequential test to resampled samples changes the effective sample size and independence assumptions.
- **What transfers:** (a) accumulation helps against diffuse, many-step attacks (decomposition, low-stakes sabotage); (b) per-context baselines cut false positives (UEBA peer groups, Dubey's per-deployment calibration); (c) robust statistics against baseline poisoning (ANTIDOTE); (d) randomized/time-varying thresholds against strategic attackers (Ghafouri et al.); (e) "impact at fixed false-alarm rate" as the metric (Urbina et al.).
- **What does not transfer cleanly:** (a) The adversary is the monitored entity itself. It produces its own baseline data, can read context about the monitor, and can inject content into the monitor's input (Terekhov et al.). UEBA and radar adversaries generally cannot write into the detector's input channel in this way. (b) There is no stable "benign" generating process: tasks differ wildly, so per-task baselines are "clutter edges" everywhere. (c) A single action can be catastrophic, so accumulation adds little for high-stakes threats. (d) Many parallel instances or sessions are a Sybil-like way to dilute per-trajectory accumulators, unless accumulation is per-model across instances (as Wen et al. do).

### Gaps
- No public evidence found of a frontier lab deploying a formal CUSUM/SPRT in production agent monitoring.
- I did not verify whether ControlArena / BashBench-style control papers (Redwood "Ctrl-Z" resampling, 2025) include cumulative-suspicion protocols beyond per-step thresholds. Worth a targeted check by another researcher.

## Q6. Radar CFAR failure modes (masking, clutter edges, interfering targets) and analogous failures in per-context baselines

### Takeaway
CFAR's documented failure modes come from the reference window being unrepresentative of the cell under test. Two cases dominate: interfering targets inflate the threshold (masking), and clutter edges cause either false alarms or masking. The classic variants (OS-, GO-, SO-CFAR) each trade masking robustness against clutter-edge robustness. Each has a direct analogue in per-context baselines for agent monitors, and the adversarial versions are worse because the agent can place its own "targets" in the reference window.

### Cited Findings
- **Clutter edge:** when a clutter edge falls inside the reference window, CA-CFAR degrades from excess false alarms on one side and masking (an inflated noise estimate and needlessly high threshold) on the other. — [IJEECS CFAR comparison (PDF)](https://ijeecs.iaescore.com/index.php/IJEECS/article/download/20542/13623); [TU Berlin commit: Detection Theory for Radar](https://commit-web.commit.tu-berlin.de/en/book/rfi/chapter/ch09/section/s05)
- **Multiple-target masking:** with two or more closely spaced targets, the stronger one biases the noise estimate upward and the weaker is missed. — [IJEECS (PDF)](https://ijeecs.iaescore.com/index.php/IJEECS/article/download/20542/13623); [rfessentials: CA-CFAR](https://rfessentials.com/resources/rf-glossary/ca-cfar/)
- **Variants and trade-offs:** OS-CFAR (Rohling 1983) sorts reference cells for robustness in multi-target and non-homogeneous backgrounds, but can produce high false-alarm rates with banded clutter. GO-CFAR handles clutter edges (it takes the larger half-window estimate) but suffers strong masking from either half. Variants "each trad[e] target-masking behavior against clutter-edge performance." — [IJEECS (PDF)](https://ijeecs.iaescore.com/index.php/IJEECS/article/download/20542/13623); [rfessentials: adaptive CFAR](https://rfessentials.com/resources/rf-glossary/adaptive-cfar/); [rfessentials: CFAR](https://rfessentials.com/resources/rf-glossary/cfar/)
- Knowledge-aided CFAR (using prior maps or side information to choose reference cells) is a patented line of work. — [US patent 9157992 "Knowledge aided detector"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9157992)

### Inferences
- **Masking ↔ contaminated baseline:** if an agent's per-task or per-agent reference distribution includes past (undetected) attack trajectories, the threshold rises and later attacks are masked. This is the CFAR version of ANTIDOTE's poisoning. Because the agent chooses what enters the reference window, it can mask deliberately. The OS-CFAR/robust-statistics fix (order statistics, trimmed means, MAD) is the standard mitigation and maps directly onto ANTIDOTE's robust PCA.
- **Clutter edge ↔ task-type boundaries:** a sliding reference window that spans a switch from routine tasks to an inherently "suspicious-looking" task class (security work, permissions changes) produces false alarms right after the switch and masking right before it. Knowledge-aided CFAR corresponds to stratifying baselines by task type with trusted labels, the same idea as UEBA's peer grouping.
- **Adversarial difference:** in radar, clutter is not strategic (deliberate jamming, i.e., ECM, is the exception). In AI-agent monitoring the "clutter" (the agent's own benign behavior) and the "target" (its attack) come from the same strategic source. The relevant precedent is therefore ECM/jamming plus security poisoning, not ordinary clutter statistics.

- I found no paper that explicitly frames LLM/agent monitor baselines as CFAR. The analogy appears unclaimed in the literature I searched (absence of evidence, limited search).
